Legality

Is drone deer recovery legal? It depends on the state

Eleven states allow it under conditions, 25 prohibit it, and 14 have not clearly said. Here is the map as we maintain it, and the written answer from Georgia DNR that moved our home state.

By the Found From Above team

Wrong in 25 states, and until this month we counted Georgia among them. That is the honest starting point for drone deer recovery in the United States: there is no national rule. Each state decides for itself whether a drone may be used to find a downed deer, and the answers range from yes with conditions, to no in any form, to a silence that no responsible operator should treat as permission.

The map as we have it

We maintain a state by state database built from statutes, regulations, and wildlife agency positions, and we check it before we sell anything. As of October 2026 it reads: 11 states allow drone deer recovery under conditions, 25 states prohibit it, and 14 states are unclear. In the unclear states we do not sell deer searches. Unclear means the agency has not said, in writing, that recovery is permitted, and "nobody has stopped us yet" is not a legal standard we are willing to fly on, for our pilots or for your harvest.

How Georgia changed

Georgia is our home state, and our first reading of its statute was that drone recovery was banned. The text of O.C.G.A. section 27-3-13 treats using a drone in connection with hunting very broadly, and we were not willing to sell Georgia deer recovery on a hopeful reading of it. So we did the unglamorous thing: we wrote to the Georgia Department of Natural Resources Law Enforcement Division and asked, in writing, how the law applies to locating downed or wounded game after the shot.

On October 19, 2026, Lt. Adam Loudermilk of the Law Enforcement Division replied in writing. Drones are permitted in Georgia to locate downed or wounded game, on these conditions: private property only; only game that was legally hunted; the landowner's permission to take off or land; never on DNR, USFS, USACOE, or other DNR managed property; the drone operator is never the taker of the game; no aiding pursuit, scouting, harassing, or taking of wildlife; and all FAA rules apply, with the Part 107 certificate as the correct certificate for the operator.

One point we state plainly everywhere we mention Georgia: that letter is Georgia DNR Law Enforcement's written enforcement position. It is not a change to the statute, and we do not present it as one. We operate exactly to the stated conditions, and if the Division's position changes, our Georgia bookings change with it.

Where recovery is treated as hunting

The prohibitions are not paperwork technicalities. In states like Michigan and Pennsylvania, regulators treat a drone used to find game, including a deer that has been shot, as part of the hunt itself, and hunting with a drone is prohibited. From that starting point, recovery is banned along with scouting, no matter that the shot has already been taken. Hunters cross state lines more than statutes do, and a method that is welcomed in Kentucky can be a violation an hour north. This is why our booking flow asks where the deer is before it asks anything else.

What "allowed with conditions" usually means

The 11 allowing states do not share one rulebook. Conditions variously cover who may fly, registration with the state program, permitted hours, whether only a deceased animal may be located, agency notification before or after the flight, and the land the flight may cover. The pattern underneath is consistent, though: the flight happens after a lawful shot, the operator is a third party rather than the hunter, and the drone is never used to find live game to hunt. When you book with us, the pilot confirms the conditions for your state before the flight, not after.

Our rule, and a suggestion

Our rule is simple: if a state has not clearly allowed drone deer recovery, we do not sell it there, and if an agency clarifies its position in writing, we update the map and our bookings the same week. If you are a hunter or a pilot reading a statute that seems ambiguous, do what we did in Georgia: write to your wildlife agency's law enforcement division, ask the specific question, and keep the answer. It is slower than assuming. It is also how a recovery method earns the trust of the people who enforce the law.

Pet and livestock searches are different: they are not hunting, and we offer them nationwide. The state by state restrictions in this article apply to deer recovery only.
