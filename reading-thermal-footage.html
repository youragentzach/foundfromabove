Warm rock, bedded deer, or your deer? Reading thermal footage

A thermal screen at night is full of bright shapes, and most of them are not deer. This is how a good pilot works a grid, throws out the false hits, and proves a location before anyone walks in.

By Zach Peterson, Founder, Found From Above

That bright spot is not always a deer, and good pilots know how to prove it before anyone walks in. Thermal footage looks simple on a phone screen: white shapes on a dark field. Reading it well is the difference between a recovery and an hour of walking to a warm rock. Here is how an experienced pilot works a night grid, frame by frame, and what has to be true before we tell a hunter, "your deer is here."

First, what the camera actually sees

A thermal camera does not see the deer. It sees heat contrast: surfaces radiating at different temperatures. Living animals stand out because they are warmer than the night around them, but they are not the only warm things in the woods. The camera also cannot see through brush. A deer under thick canopy or behind a cedar may show only in pieces, or not at all, until the angle changes. Every reading starts with those two limits in mind.

Frame one: the wide grid

The pilot flies a steady grid over the search area, high enough to see pattern, low enough to hold detail. At this height, the screen fills with candidates: bright blobs along the creek, a warm rectangle where a stump sits, two dots moving in the pasture. None of these is a conclusion. The wide pass builds a map of everything warm, so the pilot can work the list instead of chasing the first bright thing that appears.

Frame two: the ground itself is warm

Rock holds the day's sun for hours after dark. Bare dirt, gravel bars, and south facing slopes do the same, cooling slower than the grass and leaf litter around them. A sun warmed rock face can glow in the exact size range of a bedded deer, and a cluster of smaller stones can read like a body with legs tucked under it. Pilots learn the geology of a property fast, and they discount any "deer" that sits exactly on stone, faces the evening sun, and never changes shape.

Frame three: a hit is only a lead

A real deer has geometry: a body mass with a neck and head, legs when it stands, ears that move. So the pilot slows down and zooms. From a tighter angle, most false hits fall apart: the rock stays rock, the raccoon climbs, the roosted turkeys resolve into a row of smaller shapes. A bedded deer, by contrast, holds together. It has the right size for the species, the right outline, and, given a minute of watching, small signs of life or the complete stillness of an animal that is down. Even then, it is a lead, not a location. Nobody walks in on a zoom view alone.

Frame four: confirm before anyone moves

Confirmation is the step that separates a service from a guess. Where the rules allow it, the pilot confirms the animal visually with a spotlight before a location is shared. Wisconsin's guidance is the model we follow: use thermal to locate and a spotlight to confirm that the animal is killed, or reasonably presumed dead, before anyone goes to it. The spotlight answers the questions thermal cannot: species, posture, whether the animal is alive, and whether it is the deer that was shot. Only after that check does the pilot mark the spot and guide the hunter in.

What we send you, and what we do not

When a pilot confirms, you get one location and a short, honest read: what was seen, how it was confirmed, and how confident the pilot is. You do not get every bright spot on the grid, because most of them were rocks, raccoons, and warm dirt, and sending you to three of them first would waste the night and disturb the area. If nothing confirms, we say that too, and we tell you what was covered and what the limits were: rain washing out contrast, a warm night flattening it, canopy too thick to read. A thermal search improves the odds a great deal. Reading it honestly is what makes those odds real.
