Found on thermal does not mean guide the hunter to it

Finding a deer on thermal does not mean you can keep guiding the hunter toward it. In several of the states where recovery is legal, the moment the animal turns out to be alive, the lawful part of the flight is over.

By Zach Peterson, Founder, Found From Above
November 2, 2026

Here is the scene every recovery pilot knows. The grid has been running for twenty minutes and a clear deer-shaped signature lights up the screen, bedded at the edge of a thicket. The hunter, watching over the pilot's shoulder, asks the obvious question: give me the pin and walk me to it. If that deer is dead, in the right state, that is the job. If it is alive, in Kentucky and states like it, saying yes would break the law, and a professional pilot says no every time.

Kentucky draws the line at deceased

Kentucky's rule is a carve-out inside a ban. Regulation 301 KAR 3:140 prohibits using an aircraft or drone to hunt or take wildlife, or to drive, herd, or harass it. Inside that ban, the Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources permits one thing: a drone operator may notify a hunter of the location of a deceased animal. Locating live game for pursuit stays banned, and handing a hunter coordinates for a live wounded animal is prohibited. Our Kentucky page runs on exactly that line: deceased animals only.

The stop rule is bigger than Kentucky

Kentucky is not an outlier. Louisiana's 2026 to 27 regulations allow recovery of a mortally wounded deer, and state the other half plainly: if the animal is not mortally wounded, the drone must leave and share no coordinates. Wisconsin's DNR guidance allows thermal and spotlight work to locate and confirm a killed or reasonably presumed dead animal, and treats locating a live animal while hunting as a violation. Read across our legality database and the pattern is the same: the law gives back the deer that is already lost, not a tool for finishing a hunt from the air.

What the pilot does instead

  • Confirm status before anything is sharedZoom and, where the state model allows it, a spotlight check to establish whether the animal is dead before a location goes to anyone.
  • Notify only what the law allowsA deceased animal's location, in a state that permits it. Nothing that helps anyone pursue a live one.
  • End the flight when the rule says end itAnimal alive and not lawfully recoverable: the flight is over, and the honest referral is a tracking dog.

Why the line protects everyone

The hunter keeps a clean record: a recovery that followed the rule cannot be re-read later as hunting from an aircraft. The pilot keeps a certificate, a business, and a reputation with the wardens who will decide the next close call. The landowner, whose permission the whole flight depends on, learns that the drone over their fence line is doing the one job it promised to do. And the next hunter who knocks on that door benefits from the trust this one left behind.

It is fair to want the pin. A deer on a screen feels like a deer recovered. But the distance between finding and guiding is exactly where recovery law lives, and it is the distance this network is built to respect. When a pilot tells you the flight has to stop, that is not the service failing. That is the service working the way it has to, so it is still there for the next call.
